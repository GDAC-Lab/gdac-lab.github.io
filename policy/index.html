    How the lab works

    When you choose a lab, the research topic matters — but so does whether the way the lab works day to day suits you. This page describes how GDAC Lab is run.

    What the lab values

    What the lab aims at is to work control theory out properly and put it on the record, as conference talks and papers. We do not stop at “it ran” or “the numbers came out”: we make it clear what is new and why the method works.

    At the same time, laying out a route on which ordinary, steady effort produces a real result is the supervisor’s job, not the student’s. Plans here are not built on excessive workloads or unreasonable demands. The choice of topic, the seminars and the format of the meetings are all arranged with that in mind.

    In research, the answer is almost never known at the outset. You look things up for yourself, cut a large problem into pieces small enough to attack, try them, and explain what came out to other people. That way of working outlasts the particular topic you worked on.

    Research is difficult, and there will always be stretches where it does not go as planned. Even so — thinking about a hard problem, trying things, and becoming able to do something you could not do before is where the interest lies. The main goal is that nobody leaves this lab with regrets.

    Day-to-day

    Students come to the lab on weekdays as a rule, so that everyone sees the other members and the senior students. There is no core time, though: the hours you keep are up to you. If you need a weekday off, say so on the Teams channel — it is not refused.

    There are two regular fixtures.

    • A weekly meeting — everyone reports progress in three to five minutes. Slides are written in LaTeX, to get used to it. One person in turn also gives a 30–60 minute research talk, which is where the detailed discussion happens.
    • Seminars — two or three times a week in the first semester, one to two hours each. Members take turns presenting in lecture form. We read across four areas: feedback control, analytical mechanics, robust control and stability theory.

    Fourth-year students are paired with a senior student and report weekly together, working out the next week’s tasks as a pair. One-to-one meetings happen whenever they are needed.

    You are not expected to sit on a problem alone. Ask on Teams; replies are not usually slow. Fine-grained deadlines are not to my taste, but if you find it hard to get moving without one, say so and we will work that way.

    Job hunting is your own business and I do not interfere with it. Plan it as you see fit, and make the research time up when you are able to.

    How research topics are chosen

    Topics start from a broad direction suggested by the supervisor and take shape through discussion.

    A fourth-year project centres on taking control theory the lab has already built and confirming it on real hardware. By the final presentation the goals are: to explain that theory in your own words, to read one specialist book end to end, and to get an experiment working with at least one new element added to the setup. Building a new control theory yourself is not asked for at this stage; that time goes into understanding the theory properly instead.

    By the interim presentation the goals are to explain the work in terms of what the method is good for, to start working with simulations, and — with an eye on where the year is going — to complete one simple experiment that tests a component you will need.

    Conferences and papers

    Results are presented at domestic and international conferences and written up as papers.

    How to submit is taught from scratch. The difference between a journal and a conference paper, how peer review proceeds, how to treat preprints on arXiv, which sources to trust — all of it is covered inside the lab. So is how to read: skim a senior student’s thesis for orientation, then go and read the papers it was built on.

    Past work is listed on the Publications page.

    Joining the lab

    Students at Nagoya Institute of Technology

    Undergraduate lab assignment follows the department’s rules, and entry to the graduate school is decided by the entrance examination. Neither is the lab’s decision to make, so check the department’s and graduate school’s own announcements for the procedure and the dates.

    Students at overseas universities

    If you are thinking of joining from outside Japan, email me with your CV, your transcript, and a short account of what you would like to work on. I will normally suggest an online meeting before anything else, so that we can both judge honestly whether the fit is right — including whether the topic you have in mind is one this lab can support well.

    Admission itself goes through the university rather than the lab: the application routes and the entrance examination are set by Nagoya Institute of Technology, and the outcome is not mine to decide. What I can do is talk it through with you first, and point you at the right route once we know what you are aiming for.

    Visits and contact

    If you have questions about the research or about how the lab is run, get in touch at any time, whether or not assignment is close. You are welcome to visit. The contact address is on the People page, and the location is on the Access page.
